Why this lecture

The problem: same plate, a new temperature every minute

The top edge of the plate from Lecture 12 is heated and cooled during the day. The matrix AAA never changes; only the right-hand side b\mathbf{b}b does.

  • One reading per minute for a day: 1440 right-hand sides, one matrix
  • Gaussian elimination repeats the 23n3\tfrac23 n^332​n3 elimination every time, although AAA is the same
  • A realistic model with n=1000n = 1000n=1000 unknowns: 1440×0.671440 \times 0.671440×0.67 s = 16 minutes
  • Can we do the expensive part once and reuse it?

The method, step by step

Doolittle's LU method, step by step

Doolittle: LLL has ones on its diagonal. Compute row iii of UUU, then column iii of LLL, for i=1,2,…,ni = 1, 2, \dots, ni=1,2,…,n.

  1. Row iii of UUU:   uij=aij−∑k<ilikukj\;u_{ij} = a_{ij} - \sum_{k<i} l_{ik}u_{kj}uij​=aij​−∑k<i​lik​ukj​ for j≥ij \ge ij≥i
  2. Column iii of LLL:   lji=(aji−∑k<iljkuki)/uii\;l_{ji} = \big(a_{ji} - \sum_{k<i} l_{jk}u_{ki}\big)/u_{ii}lji​=(aji​−∑k<i​ljk​uki​)/uii​ for j>ij > ij>i (needs uii≠0u_{ii} \ne 0uii​=0)
  3. Forward: yi=bi−∑k<ilikyky_i = b_i - \sum_{k<i} l_{ik}y_kyi​=bi​−∑k<i​lik​yk​
  4. Backward: xi=(yi−∑k>iuikxk)/uiix_i = \big(y_i - \sum_{k>i} u_{ik}x_k\big)/u_{ii}xi​=(yi​−∑k>i​uik​xk​)/uii​
  5. For another b\mathbf{b}b, repeat only steps 3 and 4
Pseudocode
for i = 1 … n:              # factor once
    for j = i … n:
        u[i][j] ← a[i][j] − Σ_{k<i} l[i][k]·u[k][j]
    l[i][i] ← 1
    for j = i+1 … n:
        l[j][i] ← (a[j][i] − Σ_{k<i} l[j][k]·u[k][i]) / u[i][i]
for each right-hand side b:   # solve often
    y ← forward(L, b)
    x ← backward(U, y)

Same operations as elimination, organised so that each entry of LLL and UUU is computed once.

Symmetric positive definite matrices and Cholesky

Symmetric positive definite (SPD)A=ATandxTAx>0   for every x≠0A = A^T \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{x}^T A\mathbf{x} > 0 \;\text{ for every } \mathbf{x} \ne \mathbf{0}A=ATandxTAx>0 for every x=0
Then A has a "square root" factorisationA=LLT:ljj=ajj−∑k<jljk2,lij=aij−∑k<jlikljkljjA = LL^T: \quad l_{jj} = \sqrt{a_{jj} - \sum_{k<j} l_{jk}^2}, \quad l_{ij} = \frac{a_{ij} - \sum_{k<j} l_{ik}l_{jk}}{l_{jj}}A=LLT:ljj​=ajj​−k<j∑​ljk2​​,lij​=ljj​aij​−∑k<j​lik​ljk​​
  • Where SPD matrices come from: energies, covariance matrices, least squares ATAA^TAATA (Lecture 23), the plate and circuit matrices
  • Quick test: symmetric, positive diagonal, all pivots (or all ljj2l_{jj}^2ljj2​) positive
  • Only one triangle is computed: 13n3\tfrac13 n^331​n3 operations, half of LU
  • No pivoting needed: the square roots are of positive numbers and stay well-behaved

The method, step by step

Cholesky, step by step

  1. Check A=ATA = A^TA=AT
  2. For column j=1,…,nj = 1, \dots, nj=1,…,n:   ljj=ajj−∑k<jljk2\;l_{jj} = \sqrt{a_{jj} - \sum_{k<j} l_{jk}^2}ljj​=ajj​−∑k<j​ljk2​​; if the number under the root is ≤0\le 0≤0, stop: not SPD
  3. Below it: lij=(aij−∑k<jlikljk)/ljjl_{ij} = \big(a_{ij} - \sum_{k<j} l_{ik}l_{jk}\big)/l_{jj}lij​=(aij​−∑k<j​lik​ljk​)/ljj​ for i>ji > ji>j
  4. Forward: Ly=bL\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}Ly=b
  5. Backward: LTx=yL^T\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}LTx=y
Pseudocode
for j = 1 … n:
    s ← a[j][j] − Σ_{k<j} l[j][k]²
    if s ≤ 0: stop (not SPD)
    l[j][j] ← √s
    for i = j+1 … n:
        l[i][j] ← (a[i][j] − Σ_{k<j} l[i][k]·l[j][k]) / l[j][j]
y ← forward(L, b)
x ← backward(Lᵀ, y)

The method, step by step

The Thomas algorithm, step by step

  1. Store the sub-, main and super-diagonals aia_iai​, bib_ibi​, cic_ici​ and the right side did_idi​
  2. Start: c1′=c1/b1c'_1 = c_1/b_1c1′​=c1​/b1​, d1′=d1/b1d'_1 = d_1/b_1d1′​=d1​/b1​
  3. Sweep down i=2,…,ni = 2, \dots, ni=2,…,n:   w=bi−aici−1′\;w = b_i - a_i c'_{i-1}w=bi​−ai​ci−1′​,   ci′=ci/w\;c'_i = c_i/wci′​=ci​/w,   di′=(di−aidi−1′)/w\;d'_i = (d_i - a_i d'_{i-1})/wdi′​=(di​−ai​di−1′​)/w
  4. Sweep up: xn=dn′x_n = d'_nxn​=dn′​, then xi=di′−ci′xi+1x_i = d'_i - c'_i x_{i+1}xi​=di′​−ci′​xi+1​ for i=n−1,…,1i = n-1, \dots, 1i=n−1,…,1
  5. Check one equation
Pseudocode
input a, b, c, d (length n)
c′[1] ← c[1]/b[1];  d′[1] ← d[1]/b[1]
for i = 2 … n:            # down
    w ← b[i] − a[i]·c′[i−1]
    if w = 0: stop
    c′[i] ← c[i]/w
    d′[i] ← (d[i] − a[i]·d′[i−1])/w
x[n] ← d′[n]
for i = n−1 … 1:          # up
    x[i] ← d′[i] − c′[i]·x[i+1]

Two loops, no nested loop: the work grows like nnn.

Numerical (LU, Thomas)

When to use it

LU, Cholesky and Thomas: method profile

Best case
  • LU: one matrix, many right-hand sides (time steps, load cases, Newton with a frozen Jacobian)
  • Cholesky: SPD matrices, half the work and no pivoting
  • Thomas: tridiagonal systems in O(n)O(n)O(n), millions of unknowns in milliseconds
Worst case
  • LU without pivoting: zero or tiny uiiu_{ii}uii​
  • Cholesky on a matrix that is not SPD: a negative number under the root
  • Sparse matrices that are not tridiagonal: LU fills the zeros in ("fill-in")
Best suited for
  • Implicit time stepping (the same AAA every step)
  • Statistics and ML: covariance matrices, least squares, Gaussian processes (Cholesky)
  • Splines and 1-D heat or diffusion problems (Thomas)
Avoid when
  • Just one system (plain elimination is equally good)
  • Huge 2-D or 3-D grids: fill-in makes LU too big; use iterative methods (Lecture 14)
  • Forming A−1A^{-1}A−1 "to save time": LU does the same job better

Rule of thumb: tridiagonal → Thomas; SPD → Cholesky; many right-hand sides → LU once; otherwise Gaussian elimination with pivoting.
