Why this lecture

The problem: predicting a delivery time

A food-delivery company records six orders: distance xxx (km) and delivery time yyy (minutes). How long will a 15 km order take?

  • The points lie almost on a line, but no line passes through all six
  • Interpolation (Lectures 16–18) forces a curve through every point: degree 5 here
  • That curve predicts 40.4 min at 12 km and 32.9 min at 13 km: a longer trip that is faster. Nonsense
  • Data have noise; we want the trend, not every wiggle

The idea in one picture

Measured data never lie exactly on a line.Many lines look reasonable. Which one is "best"?We need a rule to score a line.Take a candidate line.For each dot, measure the vertical miss (the residual).Square each miss and add them up. Least squares picks the line with the smallest total.
In plain words
  • Real data are noisy: no line passes through every point
  • Score a line by its misses, squared and added
  • The line with the smallest score is the least-squares line

The method, step by step

Fitting a straight line, step by step

  1. Tabulate xix_ixi​, yiy_iyi​, xi2x_i^2xi2​, xiyix_iy_ixi​yi​ and add each column
  2. Write the two normal equations with these sums
  3. Solve for bbb, then a=(∑y−b∑x)/na = (\sum y - b\sum x)/na=(∑y−b∑x)/n
  4. Compute the residuals ei=yi−a−bxie_i = y_i - a - bx_iei​=yi​−a−bxi​ and S=∑ei2S = \sum e_i^2S=∑ei2​
  5. Check: ∑ei≈0\sum e_i \approx 0∑ei​≈0; look at the residual pattern; predict y^=a+bx\hat y = a + bxy^​=a+bx
Pseudocode
input x[1..n], y[1..n]
Sx ← Σ x;   Sy ← Σ y
Sxx ← Σ x²; Sxy ← Σ x·y
D ← n·Sxx − Sx²
b ← (n·Sxy − Sx·Sy) / D
a ← (Sy − b·Sx) / n
S ← Σ (y − a − b·x)²
R² ← 1 − S / Σ (y − ȳ)²
return a, b, S, R²

One pass over the data: O(n)O(n)O(n) work.

When to use it

Linear least squares: method profile

Best case
  • Many noisy measurements, a model linear in its coefficients
  • Errors roughly equal in size and independent (then least squares is statistically optimal)
  • Closed-form answer: one linear solve, no iteration
Worst case
  • Outliers or heavy-tailed errors
  • Nearly parallel columns (ill-conditioning)
  • Models nonlinear in the parameters, e.g. aebxae^{bx}aebx fitted directly
Best suited for
  • Calibration of sensors and instruments
  • Trend lines in economics and science
  • Linear regression in machine learning (the first model to try)
Avoid when
  • Exact data with no noise (interpolate instead)
  • Predictions far outside the data range
  • Data with gross errors (use robust regression)

Rule of thumb: fit, then look at the residuals before trusting R2R^2R2; solve by QR, not by forming ATAA^{T}AATA, when columns are nearly dependent.
