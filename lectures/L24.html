Why this lecture

  • Least squares from Lecture 23 needs a model linear in its coefficients
  • Idea: take logarithms, and the curve becomes a straight line

The method, step by step

Polynomial fitting, step by step

  1. Choose the degree mmm (plot the data first; start low)
  2. Tabulate the powers: ∑xk\sum x^k∑xk for k=0,…,2mk = 0, \dots, 2mk=0,…,2m and ∑xky\sum x^ky∑xky for k=0,…,mk = 0, \dots, mk=0,…,m
  3. Fill the (m+1)×(m+1)(m + 1) \times (m + 1)(m+1)×(m+1) normal equations: entry (j,k)=∑xj+k(j, k) = \sum x^{j+k}(j,k)=∑xj+k
  4. Solve for a0,…,ama_0, \dots, a_ma0​,…,am​ (Gauss with pivoting)
  5. Compute residuals, SSS, R2R^2R2; check the pattern; for new xxx evaluate by Horner
Pseudocode
input x[1..n], y[1..n], degree m
for k = 0 … 2m:  P[k] ← Σ x^k
for k = 0 … m:   r[k] ← Σ x^k·y
for j, k = 0 … m:
    M[j][k] ← P[j + k]
a ← solve(M, r)      # Gauss / Cholesky
S ← Σ (y − poly(a, x))²
return a, S

For large mmm use QR on centred, scaled xxx instead (see the limitations).

Linearisation: straighten the curve, then fit a line

Exponential: plot ln y against xy=aebx  ⟹  ln⁡y⏟Y=ln⁡a⏟A+b xy = ae^{bx} \;\Longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\ln y}_{Y} = \underbrace{\ln a}_{A} + b\,xy=aebx⟹Ylny​​=Alna​​+bx
Power law: plot ln y against ln xy=axb  ⟹  ln⁡y⏟Y=ln⁡a⏟A+b ln⁡x⏟Xy = ax^{b} \;\Longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\ln y}_{Y} = \underbrace{\ln a}_{A} + b\,\underbrace{\ln x}_{X}y=axb⟹Ylny​​=Alna​​+bXlnx​​
Saturation (Michaelis–Menten): plot 1/y against 1/xy=axb+x  ⟹  1y=1a+ba⋅1xy = \frac{ax}{b + x} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac1y = \frac1a + \frac ba\cdot\frac1xy=b+xax​⟹y1​=a1​+ab​⋅x1​
  • Fit Y=A+bXY = A + bXY=A+bX with Lecture 23's formulas, then a=eAa = e^{A}a=eA
  • Test: if the transformed points lie on a line, the model is right
  • Needs y>0y > 0y>0 (and x>0x > 0x>0 for the power law)
  • Caution: this minimises errors in ln⁡y\ln ylny, not in yyy, so large yyy get less weight

Choosing the degree

Overfitting: a perfect fit to the training data, a bad model

  • Ten noisy samples of sin⁡2πx\sin 2\pi xsin2πx; "test error" compares the fit with the true curve
  • Degree 1: underfits (train 0.62, test 0.46)
  • Degree 3–5: test error lowest (about 0.12)
  • Degree 9: passes through every point (train 0) but test error 0.58: it memorised the noise
  • Choose the degree by error on data not used for fitting

When to use it

Polynomial, exponential and trigonometric fitting: method profile

Best case
  • Smooth trends with noise; low degree (1–3)
  • Growth and decay (exponential) or scaling laws (power) seen as straight lines on log axes
  • Periodic data with a known period
Worst case
  • High-degree polynomials: ill-conditioned, wiggly, extrapolate badly
  • Log transforms with zeros or very noisy small values
  • Models that cannot be made linear in the parameters
Best suited for
  • Growth of populations, investments, epidemics (early phase)
  • Physics and engineering laws (T∝L1/2T \propto L^{1/2}T∝L1/2, Kepler T∝a3/2T \propto a^{3/2}T∝a3/2)
  • Seasonal and daily cycles
Avoid when
  • Choosing the degree by R2R^2R2 on the same data
  • Predicting far beyond the data with polynomials
  • Fitting ln⁡y\ln ylny when errors in yyy matter (use a direct fit)

Rule of thumb: pick the model from the physics or from the shape on log axes, keep the degree low, and check it on data not used for fitting.
