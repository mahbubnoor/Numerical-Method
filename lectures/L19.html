The method, step by step

Derivatives from a table, step by step

  1. Check equal spacing; note hhh
  2. Build the difference table
  3. Choose the formula: forward near the start, backward near the end, central in the middle
  4. Read the diagonal through the point x0x_0x0​ where the derivative is wanted
  5. Add the series terms 1h(Δ−12Δ2+13Δ3−⋯ )\frac1h(\Delta - \tfrac12\Delta^2 + \tfrac13\Delta^3 - \cdots)h1​(Δ−21​Δ2+31​Δ3−⋯) until they are negligible; for y′′y''y′′ use 1h2(Δ2−Δ3+1112Δ4−⋯ )\frac{1}{h^2}(\Delta^2 - \Delta^3 + \tfrac{11}{12}\Delta^4 - \cdots)h21​(Δ2−Δ3+1211​Δ4−⋯)
Pseudocode
input y[0..n] (step h), i  # point x_i
build differences d[k] = Δᵏ y_i
d1 ← 0;  sign ← +1
for k = 1 … n−i:
    d1 ← d1 + sign·d[k] / k
    sign ← −sign
d2 ← d[2] − d[3] + 11/12·d[4]
      − 5/6·d[5]          # if present
return d1/h,  d2/h²

For a point in the middle of the table, the two-point central formulas are usually enough.

When differentiation goes wrong

Limitations shown failing

  • Round-off: at h=10−11h = 10^{-11}h=10−11 the central error is 10−610^{-6}10−6, worse than at h=10−5h = 10^{-5}h=10−5 (10−1110^{-11}10−11); the second derivative is useless (Lecture 4)
  • Noise: positions with 1 mm noise (sampled every 0.01 s) give speed errors up to 0.2 m/s and acceleration errors larger than the acceleration itself (54 vs 39 m/s²)
  • Kinks: at a corner (|x| at 0) the derivative does not exist; the central formula quietly reports 0
  • Dividing by hhh (or h2h^2h2) multiplies every data error by 1/h1/h1/h (or 1/h21/h^21/h2)

When to use it

Numerical differentiation: method profile

Best case
  • Smooth functions you can evaluate anywhere: central difference with h≈10−5h \approx 10^{-5}h≈10−5 gives about 10 digits
  • Richardson: O(h4)O(h^4)O(h4) for two extra evaluations
  • Smooth, accurate tables: the difference series converges quickly
Worst case
  • Noisy measurements: errors grow like noise/h/h/h or noise/h2/h^2/h2
  • hhh too small: round-off (Lecture 4)
  • Corners and jumps: the derivative does not exist
Best suited for
  • Gradient checking in machine learning
  • Velocity and acceleration from smooth sensor data
  • Finite-difference methods for differential equations (heat, waves)
  • Jacobians for Newton's method when no formula is available
Avoid when
  • Raw noisy data (smooth or fit first, Lecture 23)
  • When an exact derivative is available cheaply (use it, or automatic differentiation)
  • High-order derivatives from tables

Rule of thumb: central differences with h≈ε1/3∣x∣h \approx \varepsilon^{1/3}|x|h≈ε1/3∣x∣; Richardson for more accuracy; smooth before differentiating data.
